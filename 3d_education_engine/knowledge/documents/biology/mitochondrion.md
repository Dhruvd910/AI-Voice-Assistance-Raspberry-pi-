## Definition
A mitochondrion (plural: mitochondria) is an organelle found in almost all eukaryotic cells, both animal and plant. It is where most of the cell's ATP is made by aerobic respiration. A typical mitochondrion is about 0.5 to 1 micrometre wide and 1 to 2 micrometres long, although shapes vary.

## Structure
It has two membranes. The outer membrane is smooth. The inner membrane is folded into cristae, which greatly increase its surface area. The space inside the inner membrane is the matrix. Mitochondria have their own small, circular DNA and their own ribosomes.

## Function
Aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose: glucose + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water, and the released energy is stored in ATP. The first stage, glycolysis, happens in the cytoplasm. The Krebs (citric acid) cycle happens in the matrix. The electron transport chain on the inner membrane pumps hydrogen ions (protons), and as they flow back through the enzyme ATP synthase, ATP is made.

## Relationships
Cells that use a lot of energy, such as heart and other muscle cells, sperm cells and liver cells, have many mitochondria.

## Misconceptions
Mitochondria do not make energy: energy cannot be created. They transfer chemical energy from food molecules into ATP. Plant cells have mitochondria too; they have chloroplasts as well, not instead.

## Facts
Mitochondria are thought to have evolved from bacteria that lived inside early cells (the endosymbiotic theory); their own DNA, ribosomes and double membrane are evidence for this.

## Questions
