## Definition
A water molecule is one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms: H2O.

## Structure
The molecule is bent, not straight. Oxygen has two bonding pairs and two lone pairs of electrons; the four electron pairs spread out roughly tetrahedrally, and the lone pairs push the bonds closer together. The measured H–O–H angle is about 104.5°, a little less than the tetrahedral 109.5°. Each O–H bond is about 0.96 Å (0.096 nm) long.

## Facts
Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen (it is more electronegative), so each O–H bond is polar. Because the molecule is bent, the bond dipoles do not cancel, and water is a polar molecule. Water molecules attract each other through hydrogen bonds, which is why water has an unusually high boiling point for such a small molecule and why ice is less dense than liquid water.

## Misconceptions
Water is not linear (180°) and its angle is not 90°. The bent shape is what makes it polar: carbon dioxide also has polar bonds but is non-polar, because it is straight.

## Questions
Why is the H–O–H angle smaller than 109.5°? Why is water polar while carbon dioxide is not?
